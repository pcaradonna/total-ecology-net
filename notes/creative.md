title: Music To Watch Seeds Grow By 009: Patricia Wolf (Yarrow)
slug: yarrow
year: 2024
medium: music / cassette
image: yarrow casette patricia wolf.jpg
link: https://patriciawolf.bandcamp.com/album/music-to-watch-seeds-grow-by-009-patricia-wolf-yarrow
link-text: pre-order

Patricia Wolf created this album in response to her artist residency at the Rocky Mountain Biological Laboratory in Gothic, Colorado, as part of the Art-Science Exchange Project in the summer of 2024. She worked closely with ecologists Dr Paul CaraDonna, Dr Amy Iler, Dr Jane Ogilvie, Dr Nickolas Waser, Dr Mary Price, and Dr Will Petry, spending weeks engaged in long-term research on plants, pollinators, and their interactions.

Each track on *Yarrow* looks at a different part of a plant's life. The songs touch on the conditions that plants need to grow, the quiet life of roots, how flowers attract pollinators, and the strength of seeds. The entire b-side features Patricia's field recordings from around Gothic, Colorado, and a guided meditation by Dr Paul CaraDonna, whose research inspired the project, inviting listeners to tune into the sounds and activities of the ecosystem.

"With this music, I hope to inspire others to slow down, appreciate plants and admire their remarkable fortitude and resilience. With such appreciation, I hope this further inspires us to protect and conserve these photosynthesizing wonders and their ecosystems for as long as the earth will hold them." — Patricia Wolf

This work was supported by the Art-Science Exchange Project at the Rocky Mountain Biological Laboratory (National Science Foundation Grant DEB-2337426).

title: On the Diet Diversity of Wild Bumble Bees
slug: bee-diet
year: 2026
medium: long-sleeve shirt
image-front: bee-diet-front-20260401-Background-Removed.png
image-back: bee-diet-back-20260401-Background-Removed.png

The northwestern grizzly bear emerges from hibernation in the spring to eat an abundance of buds and flowers, insects and salmon in the summer, and then feasts upon fruit and nuts in the fall. The adult greater sage grouse subsists on sagebrush leaves, but the young eat almost exclusively insects. Meanwhile, flower-feeding bumble bees navigate a complex and changing floral resource landscape as the needs of the colony extend across the entirety of the growing season. The composition and diversity of animal diets are essential parts of their ecology and evolution: from apex predators to insect pollinators, an animal's diet is shaped by the changing seasons, growth and development, and shifting nutritional needs. Thus, characterizing diet diversity requires a life-cycle perspective.

For most animals, however, our understanding remains coarse and temporally static. This limitation is especially pronounced for insects, including important and at-risk pollinators like bumble bees, where the lack of detailed, life stage-specific diet information constrains our ability to link resource use to population dynamics, community interactions, and conservation.

title: Perennial Plants of the Rockies
slug: plant-lifespan
year: 2025
medium: sweatshirt
image-front: perennial-plants-front-20260401-Background-Removed.png
image-back: perennial-plants-back-20260401-Background-Removed.png

*"Death is certain; the timing of death is uncertain"* (Buddhist proverb).

A fundamental component of biology is how long organisms live. This is because an organism's longevity is related to virtually all aspects of its ecology and evolution, from the ability to withstand environmental perturbations, to patterns of age-specific growth, survival and reproduction. Despite the importance of longevity—and for all its apparent simplicity—for many species it is unknown, imprecise, or misunderstood.

We are focusing on plants to ask a series of basic questions about ecological longevity: How long do perennial plants live? How best do we quantify plant lifespan? And what ecological and evolutionary factors contribute to variation in plant lifespan? To address these questions, we are using a plant demography database which includes several thousand matrix population models to calculate lifespan for 498 plant species that vary considerably in life history from across the globe.

Many have asked: "What is the question?" And for this project, the answer is: "What can mountain ball cactus teach us about being ecologists?"

*Pediocactus simpsonii* is a spherical ball shaped cactus. An adult is about the size of a grapefruit. It can withstand harsh, freezing winters in the mountains, and it persists up to about 10,000ft. From a demography perspective, *Pediocactus simpsonii* is a lovely companion: easy to demarcate individuals and easy to measure their size.

Size is measured in the field as cactus diameter (d). Given a spherical plant, volume (v) = (π/6) × d³

The official gear of the Mountain Ball Cactus Demography Society. And! All of our demography data from the first season of field work are open access on the back of the sweatshirt!
